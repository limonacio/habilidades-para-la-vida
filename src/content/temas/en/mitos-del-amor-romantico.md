## How we learn to love

According to the **theory of differential socialization** (Walker and Barton, 1983), from childhood on, family, school and culture teach us different identities according to gender, with ways of thinking, feeling and acting assigned to each one.

- **Women** were taught to be passive, dependent and obedient, to take care of others, and even to feel complete only when they "belong" to someone or are mothers (Lagarde, 2000).
- **Men** were prepared for success in public life and to not show emotions of "weakness," such as fear or sadness. Love is often left unexpressed, and shows up as domination or control.
- Fairy tales, movies and songs shape what we expect from love: they "seduce" us with an ideal of the couple (Coral Herrera).

## What a myth is

A myth is a belief presented as absolute truth, even though it has no basis. It is passed down from generation to generation and often serves to justify sexist violence. Some romantic love myths:

- **The other half:** the idea that we are incomplete halves. Each person can be a whole orange, one that does not need another to be happy.
- **Love conquers all:** the belief that love is enough to change one's partner. It keeps that hope alive and so makes it harder to leave a violent relationship or ask for help (Herrezuelo Sáez, 2013; see [The cycle of violence in relationships](/en/temas/ciclo-de-la-violencia/)).
- **If they're jealous, it's because they love me:** jealousy limits the other person's freedom; it is not a sign of love, but of **possession**.

> **Key idea**
>
> Jealousy does not measure love: it expresses the wish to possess the other person.

## Jealousy

- Behind jealousy lies a wish to have the other person completely, excluding any "rival."
- A cycle forms: the jealous person questions and controls; the other, to protect themselves, withdraws; and the jealous person reads that withdrawal as proof of their suspicion and controls even more.
- The jealous person puts so much energy into getting rid of doubt that little is left for the rest of their life, and they lose their own freedom.
- **Objectification:** when a woman is seen as an object that belongs to a man, she stops being seen as a person with will and rights (Batres, 1989). Anything that looks like a threat to that "property" triggers jealousy.

Control (checking the phone, deciding who someone talks to or how they dress) is a form of psychological violence (see [Gender-based violence](/en/temas/violencia-de-genero/)).

## How to handle jealousy

1. **Agree on boundaries** that are acceptable to both people. Each couple sets its own rules, in addition to the ones it takes from its culture (Perel, 2006). An agreement can never include control: checking the phone, forbidding friendships or deciding how the other person dresses is violence, even if it is called an "agreement."
2. **Seek a balance between security and freedom.** We all want the predictability of home and, at the same time, the autonomy to explore new things; each person needs a different amount of freedom (Mitchell, 2002).
3. **Take responsibility for your own insecurities.** Loving means accepting that we do not control what the other person feels or does. Behind jealousy there is often the need to be the most special person for the other, the fear of abandonment or betrayal, and the feeling of not being good enough: working on them is the responsibility of the person who feels them, not of their partner.
