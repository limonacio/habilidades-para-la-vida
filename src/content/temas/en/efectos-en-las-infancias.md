## Children are victims too

Gender-based violence is often thought of as something private, "between the couple." But in most cases there are children living in the middle of that violence. The forensic doctor Miguel Lorente Acosta (2001) warns that society fails to see how important it is to take them into account.

- They can be **direct victims**, when the violence is aimed at them.
- They also suffer it **indirectly**: when they witness violence between the adults raising them, or live in an environment where abuse of power is justified.
- Seeing violence teaches them a harmful model of relationships, which makes their emotional bonds harder.
- The harm is greater because the aggressor is usually a central figure for them, and because it happens at home, the place that should protect them.

> **Key idea**
>
> Ignoring the effects of violence on children makes them invisible and helps the violence repeat itself.

## Relational trauma

Sheinberg and True (2008) define it as the breakdown of the emotional or physical safety that the bond with mothers, fathers or caregivers should provide, **caused by those very same adults**. When children become aware of what is happening, they often lose their secure base, and fear, a sense of vulnerability and worry that it will happen again appear.

## Signs that may appear

1. **In social life:** isolation, insecurity, aggressiveness.
2. **At school:** problems with concentration and attention, and lower performance.
3. **Symptoms of post-traumatic stress:** insomnia, nightmares, intense fears, anxiety, a feeling of disconnection.
4. **Regressive behaviors:** acting younger than their age, wetting the bed (enuresis) or losing bowel control (encopresis) after having already achieved it.
5. **Depressive symptoms:** crying, sadness, isolation.
6. **In the emotional sphere:** difficulty expressing and managing emotions, sudden mood changes, and the risk of learning the violent model.
7. **Parentification:** taking on caregiving or protective roles that do not match their age.
8. **Normalization:** denying the violence, downplaying it or seeing it as a "normal" way of relating.

None of these signs alone proves that there is violence, and their absence does not rule it out: many children do not express it in any way.

In the most serious cases, gender-based violence ends the lives of children.

Growing up with violence is a risk factor, but it **does not condemn anyone to repeat it**: Fernández-Montalvo and Echeburúa (1997) observe that few people who grew up with violence go on to use it as adults, although they do remain more vulnerable.

## What to keep in mind when providing support

- When working with a woman experiencing violence, her children must also be included, with spaces specifically for them.
- It is important for the mother to take part in the support process, so that the children feel she is close. Fragmented interventions, or ones that cause new harm (secondary victimization), must also be avoided.
- Not all children can tell what they are going through; sometimes they do not express it in any way. That is why risk assessment looks at the type and frequency of the violence, the protection their surroundings provide, the bond with the mother, the age and each child's own resources (Barudy, 1998).

## Contact arrangements

This is one of the most debated issues, and there are two positions:

- **The one that prevails in court rulings** seeks to separate the conflict between the adults from the relationship with the children. It is guided by the **best interests of the child** (Law 26,061): it does not suspend contact with the reported father automatically, but according to the seriousness of each case, and maintains it if a good bond has been proven.
- **Another, held by professionals and associations of jurists,** argues that when there is gender-based violence, contact should always be suspended, because otherwise the adult's right is placed above the children's. Even within the judicial system, it is acknowledged that contact arrangements are often set more for the father's benefit than for the children's.

If there is a risk to the children, restraining orders can be extended to them.

In any case:

- Children must not be used as **bargaining chips** to pressure or hurt the other person: they notice everything, and that deepens their distress. Protecting children from a risky situation is not using them as bargaining chips.
- Not keeping to schedules or promises causes them mistrust and suffering.
