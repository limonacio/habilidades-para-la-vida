## What patriarchy is

Patriarchy is the social, political, cultural and economic system based on the supremacy of hegemonic masculinity and its use of power as authority. It places men in positions of privilege over women and other gender identities, and that imbalance underlies the many forms of violence against women and LGBTI+ people.

- With the rise of modern states, the power of the head of the family passed to the state and to public and private institutions, which created rules, laws, values and practices that reproduce that logic.
- Naming the system made the structural nature of violence visible: it stopped being seen as individual experiences or isolated incidents.
- **Machismo:** the everyday expression of patriarchy; sexist ideas, attitudes and behaviors that aim to keep men dominant, in public and private life. Both men and women reproduce it, because it has a strong cultural and educational component; that is why families and schools should teach non-sexist values.
- **Sexism:** attitudes and behaviors that discriminate against people because of their sex; it shows up in language, symbols and customs. It goes hand in hand with **androcentrism**, which treats the masculine as the measure of "the human" (for example, using the masculine plural in Spanish for a mixed group).
- **Gender inequality:** the social gap between men, women and gender-diverse people in access to wealth, education, decision-making positions and equally paid work. It is measured through **gender gaps**.
- **Micaela Law (27,499, passed in 2018):** makes gender and violence-against-women training mandatory for everyone working in the three branches of the national government in Argentina.

## Male privilege and learning machismo

- Although patriarchy also oppresses men, they keep their privileges intact (higher pay, normalized violence, and so on). As long as men don't let go of them, there can be no talk of a "tie" between men and women.
- Klaudio Duarte: men learn to be men; no one is born sexist. Patriarchy is reproduced through sexism, homophobia, phallocentrism and heteronormativity, and that can be unlearned: it is a political struggle.

> **Key idea**
>
> No one is born sexist: it is learned. And what is learned can be unlearned.

## "Toxic masculinity is killing men" (Kali Holloway, 2015)

- The most damaging words a boy hears: "be a man".
- The construction of femininity demands that women be thin, beautiful and helpful; the construction of masculinity forces men to constantly prove what they are.
- Its most damaging expressions: alcoholism, workaholism and violence. Even when they don't kill, they cause a kind of "spiritual death": trauma, dissociation and depression.

## Masculinization starts in early childhood

- Terry Real (*I Don't Want to Talk About It*, 1997): parents project an innate masculinity onto baby boys and, therefore, a lower need for comfort and affection.
- In fact, baby boys are more sensitive and expressive: they cry more and get more frustrated when their caregiver leaves.
- An experiment with 204 adults: shown the same crying baby, they described it as "fearful" if they thought it was a girl and "angry" if they thought it was a boy.
- Boys are talked to less, comforted less and fed less: the emotional cuts begin at the most vulnerable moment.
- Parenting emphasizes boys' achievements and competitiveness and teaches them to "control their emotions"; parents are stricter with them.
- From age 3 to 5, boys hide their feelings: they don't have fewer emotions, they learn it's better not to show them.

## Media and the "masculine mystique"

- TV shows and movies teach how men "should" be. By age 18, a young man in the U.S. has seen about 26,000 murders on screen, most of them committed by men.
- Stephanie Coontz calls this the "masculine mystique": men who are emotionally cut off and terrified of showing weakness.
- Marvin Allen: boys are encouraged to compete, focus on external achievement, endure pain and suppress vulnerability. Whoever breaks the code is humiliated.

## Consequences

- Destructive behaviors: from the socially accepted (workaholism) to the punishable (drugs, violence).
- Men are twice as likely to have anger problems and drink more alcohol, with more hospitalizations and deaths.
- In the U.S., men commit 90.5% of murders and are 76.8% of victims; they account for 80% of suicides (although suicide attempts are three to four times more common among women).
- Women tend to internalize pain and take responsibility; men tend to externalize it, toward themselves or others, as "angry victims". That externalizing masks a depression that is rarely diagnosed.

Conclusion: an unfair, unreachable standard has been set; we need to move beyond outdated ideas of masculinity and of what it means to "be a man".
