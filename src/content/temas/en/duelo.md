## Losing a bond

According to John Bowlby, human beings come into the world ready to form bonds, to love and to commit, and for that very reason also to suffer when they lose them. Our relationships are at once our greatest strength and our greatest vulnerability (see [Attachment theory](/en/temas/teoria-del-apego/)).

Bowlby (1980) points out that loss is one of the most painful experiences a human being can go through, and that witnessing it is painful too, because we feel powerless to help.

How grief is lived depends on the personality of the person going through it, the kind of bond, how the death happened, the personal history of the person going through it, and their surroundings. That is why it is important for the person to be able to **express freely** what they feel.

## The four phases

1. **Numbing:** lasts from a few hours to a week. The person may carry on almost on autopilot, tense, with outbursts of distress or anger.
2. **Yearning and searching:** lasts months and sometimes years. Crying, insomnia and restlessness appear, along with thinking about the person all the time, feeling their presence or reading signs that they have come back. **Anger** is normal in this phase: it expresses the effort to recover the lost bond.
3. **Disorganization and despair:** as the person gradually accepts that the loss is permanent, apathy and the feeling that nothing can be saved may appear.
4. **Reorganization:** the person starts to think about their new situation and to give their life a new shape.

The phases are not a fixed staircase: they overlap, and people move back and forth between them.

> **Key idea**
>
> For grief to move forward, you need to be able to feel all its emotions, including anger, instead of covering them up.

## When grief gets complicated

Bowlby described two forms of **complicated grief**: **chronic grief**, with very intense emotions that do not improve over time, and **absence of grief**, when the loss seems to have no effect. In both cases, deep down, the loss is not accepted as final and the person gets stuck in the second or third phase.

Some signs:

- Deep sadness that does not ease, isolation, loss of appetite, trouble sleeping.
- Intense guilt ("I should have prevented it") and a constant search for someone to blame.
- Feeling that a part of oneself died, that life has no meaning and that no other future is possible.

In general, around the one-year mark the person begins to show signs of recovery, although this is not a fixed deadline. Today, diagnostic manuals speak of **prolonged grief disorder** when intense yearning for, or preoccupation with, the person who died persists and affects daily life: at least 12 months in adults according to the DSM-5-TR (APA, 2022), and at least 6 months according to the ICD-11 (WHO), taking into account what is expected in each culture.

Wishing to be reunited with the person who died is common in grief. But **if thoughts of taking one's own life appear, or that wish turns into a plan, seek help right away**: in Argentina, the 135 line (free of charge) or (011) 5275-1135 and 0800 345 1435 from anywhere in the country, run by the Centro de Asistencia al Suicida; or 911 if there is immediate risk. If you are supporting someone, asking them directly whether they have thought about dying does not put the idea in their head: it helps them talk (WHO, 2014).

Some things help prevent grief from getting complicated: giving special support to whoever cared for the person during an illness, telling the whole family what happened (children too, in words suited to their age) and avoiding blaming others or oneself.

## What rituals are for

The anthropologist Raymond Firth pointed out that funeral rituals do not benefit the person who died, but those who remain. They serve three functions:

1. **They help family and close ones:** they show that the loss is real, allow grief to be expressed in public and give the bereaved a new place in the community.
2. **They let the community know:** they allow others to say goodbye and express their own emotions.
3. **They strengthen mutual help:** exchanges of support between families are a form of reciprocal altruism (Trivers, 1971): today I help, and tomorrow, if I need it, others help me.
