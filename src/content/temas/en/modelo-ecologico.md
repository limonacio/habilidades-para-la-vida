## Bronfenbrenner's ecological theory

Urie Bronfenbrenner's Ecological Systems Theory is an environmental approach to human development: it looks at the different settings a person lives in and how they shape that person's change and development.

> **Key idea**
>
> Violence is not an individual phenomenon but a social one: ending it requires action by the State together with civil society.

## Why a model matters

- A model is an abstract, conceptual, graphic or visual representation of interconnected phenomena, systems or processes; it helps analyze, describe, explain, simulate and explore the elements of a phenomenon.
- In public policy, "model" refers to the set of components a law or policy should include: the theoretical and technical elements that define the problem, and proposals for organizing actions and distributing resources (authority, economic, human and material).
- Central idea: violence is not an individual phenomenon but a social one, and action by the State together with civil society is essential to end it.

## The ecological model for a life free of gender-based violence

- It is a conceptual, technical and methodological framework for addressing gender-based violence (GBV).
- It starts from the recognition that GBV is a complex, multifaceted problem with biographical, psychological, social and environmental roots, with no simple or single solution.
- It calls for multidisciplinary approaches and interventions on several levels, with several public policy sectors working at once (PAHO, 2002; UN, 2006).
- Knowing precisely the conditions behind violent practices at each level makes it possible to prevent, respond to and punish violence, and to build safe conditions for everyone.

Instruments the model draws on:

- Convention of Belém do Pará (1994): Inter-American Convention on the Prevention, Punishment and Eradication of Violence against Women.
- Declaration on the Elimination of Violence against Women (United Nations, 1993).
- Bogotá Declaration: Safe Cities for Women and Girls (2004).
- In Argentina: Law 26,485 and Regulatory Decree 1011/10.

## The ecological model in schools

- It makes it possible to move from the causes and forms of school bullying to the question of what meaning the people involved give to it.
- For Bronfenbrenner, we need to understand the macro-, meso- and microsystem: the ecological environment extends far beyond the immediate situation affecting the person.

## Heise's ecological approach and its levels of analysis

Lori Heise (1998) proposed the ecological approach to violence based on Bronfenbrenner (1979). It assumes that each person is embedded in multiple relational levels (individual, family, community and society) where different forms of violence can arise. The WHO adopted it in 2003.

- **Microsocial level – individual:** biosocial factors in a person's history: age, sex, education, employment, income. Risk factors: a history of aggressive or self-deprecating behavior, personality disorders, addictions, crises caused by job loss, professional frustration or school failure.
- **Microsocial level – relationships:** the closest relationships: family, partner, close friends. Growing up in violent family settings, being attacked or witnessing violence can predispose a person to suffer or commit violence in adolescence, youth and adulthood.
- **Mesosocial level:** community settings: neighborhood, school and workplace. Risks increase with urban decay, overcrowding, economic hardship, unemployment, lack of educational and sports opportunities, lack of recreational spaces, and the presence of crime, vandalism or homicide.
- **Macrosocial level:** broad features of the social structure that encourage or inhibit violence: impunity, easy access to weapons, a culture of lawlessness, corruption among security and justice officials, lack of respect for institutions. They work as "negative or anomic social capital".
- **Historical level (chronosystem):** the historical moment in which violence occurs: the motivations of the time and the social and symbolic meaning of violence (for example, the force of inherently violent ideologies).

## What the approach is for

- It analyzes the determinants and risk factors that affect the dynamic relationship between people and their environment.
- It helps identify the roots of what prevents, delays or fosters a climate of violence, and the factors that can support change.
- It helps understand family and community conflicts as a structural part of social violence, and design coherent lines of action.
