## Why they matter

- Relationships give us the capacity to love, companionship and support; to sustain them, we need to learn interpersonal skills.
- The most important ones: assertiveness, knowing how to ask, knowing how to say no, and negotiating conflict without damaging the relationship.

> **Key idea**
>
> Both passivity and aggression damage relationships; assertiveness lets you take care of your own needs without running over the other person.

## Paying attention

- Notice the other person's feelings and reactions, observe yourself, and pay attention to verbal and body language and tone of voice.
- Be in the here and now: don't think about what you'll say next or get lost in memories. Paying attention helps prevent conflict.
- Consequences of not paying attention in a couple: not knowing the other's needs, projecting your own fears, and exploding or running away when faced with a negative response you could have seen coming.

## Passive and aggressive behavior

- **Passive:** giving in and giving up your own needs creates frustration and resentment; in the short term it seems to protect the relationship, but in the long run it makes it unsustainable (you explode, get depressed or pull away).
- **Aggressive:** it destroys relationships. It comes from a strong sense of "how things should be" and a need to control.
- **Assertiveness:** the tool for resolving both.

## The two balances in a relationship

- **"I want – others want":** every relationship is two people trying to meet their needs. Without balance, the relationship becomes unstable. You need to know and say what you want, find out what the other person wants, negotiate and give what you can.
- **"Want – should":** focusing only on what you want breeds resentment in the other person; loading yourself with "shoulds" turns the relationship into a burden you want to escape.

## Social skills and communication

- Social skills: communication, assertiveness, empathy. Thinking skills: problem solving and creative thinking.
- Aspects of communication: body language (fidgeting with objects shows nervousness; a handshake conveys confidence), observing, and speaking carefully.
- Keys: speak with your body (smile, make eye contact, warm tone); use opening phrases ("I'm listening", "tell me more"); clear words and short sentences; be concrete and direct; avoid words that hurt or shut down the conversation; ask when in doubt; paraphrase ("let me see if I understood…").
- Assertiveness: saying what we feel or think in a kind, simple way, taking others into account; especially important for children and teenagers facing peer pressure.

## A problem-solving framework

1. Recognize the problem.
2. Evaluate alternatives: analyze the pros, cons and consequences of each ("what do I gain and what do I lose?").
3. Choose the best option: the one with the most benefits, ruling out those that aren't feasible.
4. Put it into practice: tell the people involved and have a detailed step-by-step plan ("what do I need?").
5. Review the results: if they're negative, check where the mistake was or look for another solution.
