## Brain, evolution and violence

- The human brain is the most complex system we know; to understand it, we need to look for patterns and simple explanations.
- Human evolution began at least 6 million years ago, when a population of African primates split into two lineages.

> **Key idea**
>
> Secondary emotions, the "feelings about feelings", tend to cause more problems than primary ones.

## The three-brain model (MacLean)

- **Reptilian complex:** the most primitive structure (basal ganglia, brainstem and cerebellum). Immediate survival functions; simple, impulsive and stereotyped behaviors depending on the body's state (fear, hunger, anger). Responses: fight, flight or freeze.
- **Limbic system:** it appears with early mammals; responsible for emotions linked to experience and for learning: we repeat what brings pleasant emotions and avoid what brings pain.
- **Neocortex:** the most recent and complex structure: it allows us to learn the nuances of reality, to plan, and to think systematically and logically, independently of emotions.
- **Current view:** neuroscience now holds that what matters is less the function of each part on its own than how the parts connect and work together; and that evolution did not happen in neat stages.

## Primary and secondary emotions

- Emotions are electrical and chemical signals that tell us what is happening outside; they start in the senses and are processed in the limbic system.
- **Primary:** the first reaction to an event (sadness at a death, anger at an insult).
- **Secondary:** reactions to primary emotions, "feelings about feelings" (anger, then guilt). They cause more problems because they chain negative emotions together.
- Both need to be addressed to avoid harmful coping strategies.
- **Ambivalence:** more than one emotional reaction to the same event, pulling in different directions (e.g., excitement and anger when an absent father reappears).

## Analyzing an emotional situation: six questions

Example: Marcela is overwhelmed when she finds her husband drunk, once again.

1. **What happened?:** describe the situation that led to the emotion.
2. **Why do you think it happened?:** identify the causes you attribute to it; the meaning you give it shapes your emotional reaction.
3. **How did it make you feel, emotionally and physically?:** identify the primary emotion (anger) and the secondary ones (hopelessness, shame), and the body sensations (muscle tension, nausea).
4. **What did you want to do?:** recognize the urge. We don't always act on it: noticing that you controlled one urge gives hope that you can control others.
5. **What did you do and say?:** what you actually did as a result of the emotion.
6. **How did it affect you later?:** the long-term consequences (in this case, a penalty at work).

## Exercise 1: describing an emotion

- Choose one pleasant and one unpleasant emotion from a list (irritated, happy, jealous, vulnerable, etc.).
- Draw what it looks like, and give it a sound and an action (bored: taking a nap; shy: hiding).
- Describe its intensity creatively ("my heart is like a drum") and its overall quality ("knees like jelly", "water about to boil").
- Complete "my emotion makes me think that…": separating thoughts from emotions gives you more control over both.

## Exercise 2: shifting focus (mindfulness)

An exercise of about 10 minutes to separate your present experience from what is happening emotionally. You alternate attention between the emotion and each sense:

1. Eyes closed: name the emotion and give it a shape.
2. Sight: silently describe an object in the room (color, shape, weight).
3. Back to the emotion: give it a sound and an action.
4. Hearing: notice even the faintest sounds.
5. Back to the emotion: describe its intensity and quality.
6. Smell: notice any smells, or the flow of air through your nose.
7. Back to the emotion: notice the thoughts linked to it.
8. Touch: touch a nearby object; finish with three to five slow, deep breaths.
