## Starting point

Sometimes we have very painful feelings or destructive impulses that make our emotional life and relationships harder. These emotions "try" to handle complex situations, but they produce irrational feelings that we tend to repress or censor. Internal Family Systems (IFS) is a model for understanding how the mind works.

> **Key idea**
>
> Every part of us, even the one that causes the most trouble, is trying to protect us from some kind of harm.

## The mind as an internal family

- The mind is not a single unit: it is a system of interacting parts, each with "a mind of its own".
- It is pictured as an internal family: wounded children, impulsive teenagers, rigid adults, overly critical parents, worried friends, supportive relatives.
- The approach seeks to find the center, identify the parts that cause problems in relationships, heal them and bring them together.
- All parts are accepted and welcomed, with understanding, warmth and trust, so they can interact in a healthy way.

Each part becomes active at certain moments, and the important thing is to recognize it. Examples: with a stranger, the shy part wants to disappear; when criticized, a part feels incompetent; when rejected by a partner, the desolation of an abandoned child appears.

## The Self

- It is the key to healing and to integrating the parts, through compassion, curiosity and connection.
- Mental balance means recognizing the parts and integrating them into the Self.
- By nature, the parts have positive qualities (joy, freedom, perception, creativity) that they lost because of childhood wounds.
- Everything a part does has a positive purpose: to protect from harm or to help us feel better.
- When a part is activated, it tries to take control away from the Self and take over its role.

Functions of the Self: connecting with others in a harmonious way; being curious (open to accepting without criticizing or judging); being compassionate toward pain; and staying calm in complex situations.

## The internal system: protectors and exiles

What other theories call "defense mechanisms", this model calls subpersonalities or parts: entities with their own feelings, beliefs, motivations and memories.

- **Protectors:** they try to keep pain from being triggered. They organize life and the mind to keep us in a comfort zone, free of fear or shame. Some block internal pain; others try to control the outside world. They don't distinguish between past and present.
- **Exiles:** the part of the child who has been suffering since the past. They got stuck in time because something very painful happened and there were no emotional resources to cope with it (a chaotic or disorganized family, or events beyond the family's control that were never processed).

How protectors work:

- **Distracting from pain:** overworking, using substances, overspending (when we are rejected, criticized or ignored).
- **Criticizing to protect:** controlling us so we are "a good child", or pushing us to be more productive and successful to avoid criticism from others.
- **Criticizing to boost self-esteem:** becoming charming or pleasant so that others won't hurt or abandon us.
- **Taking care of others:** making the care of others the most important thing in life.

## How to know which part to work with

You identify the part that plays an extreme role and causes an out-of-proportion or dysfunctional reaction, by looking at three areas:

- **Feelings:** sadness, anger or fear; feeling critical, judgmental, rash, petty, guarded, and so on.
- **Body sensations:** physical ones (stomachache, muscle tension) in one area or the whole body; the Self, by contrast, feels at peace, open and energized.
- **Thoughts:** the ones that flow about another person's behavior or about ourselves.

## Separating from a protector

- Centering means placing yourself in the Self: recognizing the part that is affecting you and, from the Self, focusing on it to understand it, accept it and calm it.
- You shouldn't merge with that part, or avoid, deny or censor it: it is saying something about your personal history.
- You ask it to step aside for a moment so you can understand it; you listen to it, learn its reasons, and that awakens the curiosity and compassion needed to heal.

## Caring for the inner child: reparenting the exile

- It is not the experiences themselves that determine how we feel, but the emotional residue they left.
- The Self can give the exile a new, positive experience to replace the painful one.
- You work with imagination: revisiting the painful experience through adult eyes, connecting with the child, asking what they need and giving it to them (using all the senses).
- Redoing the scene: in your imagination, explaining to the parents that this is just a child and what the child needs to feel okay.
- Exploring the blocks that keep the exile from accepting the reparenting, and letting yourself be carried by the positive emotions.
- At the end, taking the exile to a safe, comfortable imaginary place where they can count on you.

## The unburdening ceremony

- It completes the work with exiles: an inner ceremony in which the exile releases its burden and is transformed.
- First, an inner dialogue checks whether the exile is willing to trust the Self.
- You locate where the burden is held in the body (pain, tension, a "knot" of anxiety in the stomach).
- You ask the exile which element of nature it wants to give the burden to (air, light, water, fire or earth): something powerful and primal carries it away or transforms it. You can also "write down" negative beliefs and throw them into the fire, always in your imagination.
- When the exile feels joy, strength, freedom and love, allow yourself to enjoy it.
