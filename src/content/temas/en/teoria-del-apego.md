## What it is

The English psychiatrist and psychoanalyst **John Bowlby** (1907-1990) studied why children become emotionally attached to the people who care for them, and what happens when they are separated from them.

- At birth we cannot survive or regulate our emotions on our own. We learn to do so in the bond with whoever cares for us and responds to our signals (Fonagy, 2004).
- For Bowlby, attachment is an instinctive behavior that is gradually shaped in our relationships with others.
- From those first experiences come ideas about ourselves, about others and about relationships, which influence our relationships in adult life.
- He drew on animal studies: Konrad Lorenz showed that baby birds form a strong bond with the first moving figure they see, and Harry Harlow, in experiments with monkeys, showed the need for contact and comfort, beyond food.

> **Key idea**
>
> When a child trusts that their caregiver will be available, they feel less afraid and dare to explore the world.

## The three keys

- **Support:** knowing that the attachment figure will be there when needed.
- **Trust:** that sense of security is built little by little in childhood and tends to last a lifetime.
- **Accessibility:** how much we expect the caregiver to be available and to respond; that expectation gradually shapes our character.

## Attachment styles

By observing how babies reacted when separated from and reunited with their mother, the psychologist **Mary Ainsworth** (1978), a collaborator of Bowlby's, described one secure style and two insecure ones:

- **Secure:** the caregiver recognizes the baby's needs and responds quickly and with affection. It fosters emotional stability, initiative and self-confidence. In adulthood it makes satisfying relationships easier.
- **Ambivalent (or resistant):** the caregiver responds inconsistently, sometimes with warmth and other times with indifference. Insecurity, a constant demand for affection and low frustration tolerance predominate.
- **Avoidant:** the caregiver rejects or ignores the baby's needs, with little physical contact. The baby learns to suppress their feelings and to avoid contact so as not to be rejected; in adulthood, close relationships may be hard for them.

Later, Main and Solomon (1990) added a fourth style, **disorganized**, which is common when the figure who should provide security is also a source of fear, as in situations of abuse, though not only in them.

## It is not destiny

Attachment styles describe tendencies, not fixed labels. Abuse in childhood has an impact on adult life, but recognizing it makes it possible to seek help, to change, and to learn to take care of ourselves and of the people we love. Later relationships (a partner, friendships, a therapeutic space) can also provide security.
